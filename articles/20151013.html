FORBES · October 13, 2015

Voters Should Be Fed Up That No One Is Talking Monetary Policy

By Johannes K. Schmidt

Read the original ↗

Tonight, the first Democratic primary debate will be held at the Wynn Las Vegas hotel. There, party front-runner Hillary Clinton will face off against her top challenger, Senator Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.) and other lesser known candidates such as former Maryland Governor Martin O’Malley, former Rhode Island Governor Lincoln Chafee and former Virginia Senator Jim Webb. Of course, the idea of Vice President Joe Biden joining the candidates on stage after a last minute announcement is a very real possibility.

On stage, these candidates will likely tackle the hot-topic issues that most appeal to their bases, including income inequality, the environment and Planned Parenthood. But, unfortunately for sound money advocates, the candidates will likely ignore monetary policy – an issue with serious consequences for all Americans, whether they realize it or not.

Money is our society’s most basic medium of exchange. It affects our most basic human interactions, both in the short and long terms. Decisions taken by the Fed directly impact the value of the dollars we use to buy a gallon of milk today or pay off a mortgage over the next thirty years. When the Fed clearly articulates its policy objectives and then takes the necessary but well-established steps to achieve those objectives, Americans can productively allocate their time and resources. Monetary policy along these lines would result in a more prosperous future for Americans. Experimental, discretionary monetary policy, by contrast, leaves Americans uncertain about the future – and, hence, unsure how much to save and invest towards bringing that future about.

If monetary policy is so important, why have presidential candidates been largely silent on the matter? In the Republican debates that have taken place in the last two months, not a single candidate has mentioned the Federal Reserve. This is especially surprising considering the September debate took place on the eve of the Federal Open Market Committee’s decision to maintain its current interest rate target.

As Dr. Judy Shelton, codirector of the Sound Money Project, notes at The Hill, candidates “should be prepared to talk about the impact of next-to-nothing returns on savings accounts for retirees; they should be able to explain how the Fed's policies have discouraged loans to consumers and small business owners. Someone needs to point out that income inequality has been exacerbated by easy money, which benefits wealthy corporations and investors.” More likely, it seems, candidates will remain silent as the Fed continues its reckless course.

Really, anybody who aspires to lead this country should be prepared to explain to the American people why they did not sound the alarm when a magnanimous Fed finally drives the global economy off a cliff.

But of course, I should be clear: It is not the role of politicians to micromanage the Fed. A Fed governed by the whims of politics would be a monetary disaster. The politicized central banks of Argentina and Venezuela are clear examples of this. Instead the President should call on Congress to set a clear, obtainable rule for the Fed and then hold the Fed accountable for achieving its objective. One step in the right direction might be redefining the Fed’s dual-mandate, as Jared Meyer and I recently suggested. But while economists continue to argue over which rule might be optimal, politicians should take part in the conversation by informing the public of the importance of a disciplined, rule based Fed.

When the Fed oversteps its bounds and endangers the value of the dollar and the economy as a whole, politicians must be there to demand an explanation on behalf of the American people.

Democrats might do well tonight in noting that the income gap is actually expanded when the Fed prints cheap dollars that mostly benefit corporations and those in a position to reinvest their money. At the October 28 Republican debate – coincidentally on the same day that the FOMC will announce if it will raise rates or not – Republicans would do well in recognizing that this country was founded on the premise that, as Jefferson alluded to in his Notes on the Establishment of a Money Unit, sound money is a necessary condition of a free society. They should make it clear to the American people that the Fed cannot remedy the fiscal maladies that ail our economy.

But really, any mention or allusion to the Federal Reserve from any of the candidates would be a positive step in the right direction.

As citizens, we must strive to elect a candidate that is willing fight for the dignity of our currency and champion the idea that if we wish to once again experience strong economic growth, rules must replace discretion in monetary policy. The importance of electing such an individual cannot be understated; after all, the next President will have the power to decide who takes the reins of the Federal Reserve in 2018.
